The more deductible expenses you have and can justify, the less profit gets taxed. Knowing what counts as deductible usually isn't the problem — properly justifying it if the Tax Agency reviews it is.

## The general rule

An expense is deductible if it's necessary for your business, tied to it, and you can justify it with a full invoice in your name. A receipt or a bank transfer note on its own usually isn't enough proof.

## Common deductible expenses

- Materials and stock purchases.
- Utilities and rent on your premises, or the proportional part if you work from home (covered in [deducting home expenses if you work remotely](/en/blog/deducir-gastos-vivienda-teletrabajo)).
- Your social security contributions and insurance tied to your activity.
- Accounting, legal, or other professional services you hire.
- Advertising, your website, and the software tools you use to work.
- Training directly related to your activity.
- Your vehicle, but only if it's "exclusively used" for the business — transport, sales reps, taxis. This is one of the more nuanced expenses and worth reviewing case by case.

## What usually raises questions

Meals and client expenses are deductible, but with limits, only when directly tied to your business activity, and always paid by card or transfer — cash payments above a certain amount lose their validity as proof.

## The costliest mistake

It's rarely over-deducting. The common mistake is not keeping invoices properly, or paying in cash for something that should have a bank trail. The Tax Agency tends to challenge the proof more than the concept of the expense itself.

If you're unsure about a specific expense, it's better to ask before deducting it than to risk it being questioned later — we review this with you case by case.
