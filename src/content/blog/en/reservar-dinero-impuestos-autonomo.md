One of the most common shocks when you start out as self-employed in Spain isn't paying taxes — it's not having the money set aside when it's due. The problem is almost never a tax problem, it's a cash-flow problem.

## Why you need a reserve

When you get paid on an invoice, part of that money was never really yours: the VAT you charge isn't income, it's money you're holding for the Tax Agency until you settle it on model 303. If you mix it in with day-to-day cash, you can end up short at the quarterly deadline even if the business is doing fine.

## A rough reference

As a general rule, it's reasonable to set aside between 15% and 30% of your gross invoicing, depending on how many deductible expenses you have and your specific situation. It helps to treat it as two separate pots:

- **VAT**: set it aside as soon as you collect it. It's not an estimate — it's an exact figure you already know the moment you invoice.
- **Income tax**: a percentage of your net profit (see [how quarterly income tax is calculated](/en/blog/calculo-irpf-trimestral)), which is an estimate until the quarter closes.

## How to do it without overcomplicating it

A separate account that automatically receives a percentage every time you invoice is the simplest way to make sure the reserve doesn't depend on your discipline. Reviewing the real figure with us every quarter, instead of guessing, avoids both falling short and over-reserving money that's sitting idle for no reason.

## Setting money aside isn't the same as planning

Reserving a percentage protects you at the quarterly payment, but it doesn't solve uneven income across the year. If your invoicing varies a lot month to month, it's worth also looking at how to spread that income out so you don't hit year-end with surprises — and that, in turn, affects how much of a reserve you actually need during the slow months.
