## Two different snapshots of the same business

The balance sheet and the profit and loss account (P&L) aren't competing with each other: they answer different questions. The balance sheet is a **fixed snapshot** at a specific moment ("what do you have and owe today?"). The P&L is a **film** of a period ("what came in and went out over these months, and what profit is left?"). You need both to get a real picture of how your business is doing.

## The balance sheet, in three blocks

- **Assets**: everything the business owns (cash in the bank, invoices pending collection, equipment, premises, stock...). Ordered from most liquid (cash) to least liquid (fixed assets).
- **Liabilities**: everything the business owes to others (suppliers, loans, the tax office, social security).
- **Equity**: what's left once you subtract liabilities from assets — in simple terms, the part of the business that's actually "yours".

The equation that always holds is Assets = Liabilities + Equity. If a balance sheet doesn't add up, there's an error somewhere, not an exception to the rule.

## The P&L, from top to bottom

The profit and loss account starts from your income and subtracts costs in blocks until it reaches the final profit (or loss):
1. Sales revenue.
2. Minus the direct cost of what was sold → gross margin.
3. Minus overheads (rent, utilities, accounting fees, marketing...) → operating result.
4. Minus financial expenses (loan interest) and adjustments → result before tax.
5. Minus the corresponding tax (personal income tax if you're self-employed under direct assessment, corporate tax if you have a limited company) → net result for the year.

## The first thing worth checking in each one

On the balance sheet: whether your current assets (what you can turn into cash within a year) cover your current liabilities (what you have to pay within a year). If they don't, you have a liquidity problem even if the business is profitable on paper.

On the P&L: whether the operating result (before interest and tax) is positive. A business can show a final profit thanks to a one-off item (selling an asset, for example) and still have a structural problem in its core activity — the operating result is what tells you whether the "day-to-day" business actually works.

## A simple example, with both documents at once

Imagine a quarter with these numbers:

**P&L for the quarter**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Sales revenue | €12,000 |
| Direct cost of goods sold | −€3,000 |
| **Gross margin** | **€9,000** |
| Overheads (rent, accounting, marketing...) | −€4,000 |
| **Operating result** | **€5,000** |
| Financial expenses | −€200 |
| Result before tax | €4,800 |
| Tax (approx.) | −€960 |
| **Net result for the period** | **€3,840** |

**Balance sheet at quarter-end**

| Assets | | Liabilities + Equity | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | €2,000 | Suppliers | €1,200 |
| Accounts receivable (unpaid invoices) | €3,500 | Loan | €2,000 |
| IT equipment | €1,500 | Equity | €3,800 |
| **Total assets** | **€7,000** | **Total** | **€7,000** |

The quarter closes with €3,840 in profit — good news on paper. But the balance sheet shows the other side: of that €7,000 in assets, only €2,000 is actually cash; €3,500 is invoices issued and still unpaid. If that client is slow to pay, the business could run into a liquidity problem next month even though the P&L says it was profitable. That's exactly the kind of combined reading these two documents allow, which neither one gives you on its own.
