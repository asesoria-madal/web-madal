A **single-member SL** (SLU, *sociedad limitada unipersonal*) is a Spanish limited company with only one partner, who can be a person or a company. It works like any SL: it has its own tax ID, its own accounts and pays corporate income tax, and the partner is only liable for its debts up to what they put in. The difference lies in three obligations the law adds because there is a single partner.

## How is a single-member SL different from being self-employed?

As a freelancer (*autónomo*), you and your business are the same person: you are liable for debts with everything you own and pay personal income tax, which rises by bracket. With a single-member SL, the company is a separate legal person: it is liable with its own assets and pays corporate income tax at a flat rate.

| | Self-employed | Single-member SL |
|---|---|---|
| Liability for debts | Unlimited, with your own assets | Limited to what you put in (if the obligations are met) |
| Tax on profits | Personal income tax, progressive | Corporate income tax, flat rate |
| Setting up | Registration with the tax office and Social Security | Deed before a notary and Companies Registry registration |
| Accounting | Record books | Full accounts, filed with the Registry every year |
| Social Security | Self-employed scheme (RETA) | RETA as a company self-employed worker, if you work in it |

When it pays to switch from self-employed to an SL depends mainly on profit and how much you need to take out each month. We explain it in [self-employed or SL: when to switch](/en/blog/autonomo-vs-sl-cuando-pasar).

## What obligations does a single-member SL have?

1. **Register single-member status with the Companies Registry.** If the company is born with one partner, the deed says so. If it ends up with one partner later (for example, because you buy out your partner), this must be declared in a public deed and registered.
2. **State it on every document.** The company must show that it is single-member on its correspondence, invoices, orders and legal notices. That is why "Sociedad Unipersonal" or "SLU" is added to the name.
3. **Keep a register of contracts with the sole partner.** Contracts between the company and its partner (a loan, a lease, the director’s contract…) are recorded in a dedicated book and mentioned in the notes to the annual accounts.

The sole partner also acts as the general meeting, and their decisions are recorded in minutes signed by them or by the director.

## What happens if single-member status is not registered?

This is the costliest mistake. If **six months** pass from the moment the company has a single partner without registering it with the Companies Registry, the partner becomes **personally, jointly and unlimitedly** liable for the debts the company takes on during that time. In other words, they lose the very protection an SL is set up for.

## How much capital is needed?

The same as any SL: from **€1**. But below €3,000, at least 20% of profit has to go to a legal reserve, and if the company is wound up the partner is liable for the difference up to €3,000. We cover it in [minimum capital for an SL](/en/blog/capital-minimo-sl).

## Does the sole partner have to be self-employed?

If they work in the company and control it, yes: they register with the RETA as a **company self-employed worker**, even without a salary. With one partner, control is total. If they only contribute capital and do not work in it, no. See [company self-employed vs. the general scheme](/en/blog/autonomo-societario-vs-reta-general).

## Can it stop being single-member?

Yes. If a new partner comes in, the loss of single-member status is declared in a deed, registered, and "Unipersonal" is removed from the name. The same applies the other way round: if the company is left with one partner, this has to be declared and registered.

## In short

A single-member SL gives you an SL’s limited liability without needing partners. In return, there are three formalities: register single-member status, show it on documents and keep the register of contracts with the partner.
