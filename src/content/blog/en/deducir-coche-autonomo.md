It's one of the most frequent questions, and the answer tends to disappoint: for income tax (IRPF), most self-employed workers can't deduct anything for the car; for VAT, half. They are two taxes with different rules, and it's worth handling them separately.

## Income tax: all or nothing

For IRPF, the car and all its related costs (fuel, insurance, repairs, depreciation, financing interest, parking, tolls) are only deductible if the vehicle is used **exclusively** for the business. If you also use it for anything personal, even a weekend trip to the shops, it stops qualifying and none of those costs are deductible.

There's no partial percentage here like there is for [your home when you work remotely](/en/blog/deducir-gastos-vivienda-teletrabajo). It's all or nothing: either the car is for work only, or for income tax it counts as zero.

## The exceptions where business use is presumed

The rules allow occasional personal use (and keep the deduction) only in a closed list of activities:

- Mixed-use vehicles for transporting goods.
- Passenger transport for a fee (taxi, ride-hailing).
- Driving instruction (driving schools).
- Travel by commercial agents or sales representatives.
- Vehicles used for rental.

If your activity is on that list, you deduct the car and its costs normally. If not, for income tax you start from zero.

## VAT: 50% with barely any argument

VAT works differently. The law presumes that a vehicle used in the business is **50%** business-use, so you can deduct half of the input VAT: on the purchase (or on the lease or long-term rental payments) and also on fuel, repairs and tolls.

You can deduct more than 50% if you can prove greater business use, and Hacienda can reduce it if it proves otherwise. In practice, 50% is the figure that holds up without having to justify anything. The same activities from the list above carry a 100% presumption, and for VAT the list also adds security vehicles and those a manufacturer uses for tests or demonstrations.

In every case you need a complete invoice in your name and the vehicle registered as used for the business.

## How business use is proven

To defend exclusive business use for income tax, or a percentage above 50% for VAT, it helps to have the vehicle sign-written, an insurance policy that declares professional use, a separate car for private use, and a log of visits or mileage. None of this guarantees the deduction, but it counts if Hacienda asks.

## A worked example

Car worth €20,000 + VAT (€4,200) = €24,200, with €1,200 + VAT (€252) in fuel per year.

| | Activity outside the exceptions list (mixed use, the most common case) | Activity on the list (taxi, sales rep...) |
|---|---|---|
| Income-tax deduction on the purchase | €0 — not exclusively business-use | Full depreciation under the tables |
| Income-tax deduction on annual fuel | €0 | €1,200 in full |
| Deductible VAT on the purchase | €2,100 (50% of €4,200) | €4,200 (100%) |
| Deductible VAT on annual fuel | €126 (50% of €252) | €252 (100%) |

For most self-employed workers, the entire saving from "buying a car to write it off" comes down to that €2,100 of VAT on the purchase and 50% of the VAT on fuel: nothing on income tax. If your activity is on the exceptions list, the calculation changes completely.

## Before buying a car "to write it off"

Run the real numbers: for most self-employed workers the saving is limited to 50% of the VAT on the purchase and on maintenance, not the whole car. If your activity fits one of the exceptions, it's a different story.
