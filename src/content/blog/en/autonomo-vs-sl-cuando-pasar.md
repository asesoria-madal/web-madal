There's no rule that says "past this turnover, switch to an SL." The decision comes down to three things: how much you invoice, how much risk you're carrying, and how much tax you'd pay under each structure. Here they are, one at a time.

## The rough figure people cite

Several industry sources agree that it usually starts to pay off somewhere between €60,000 and €80,000 in annual turnover, though that's not a figure set by the Tax Agency or by law — it's a practical reference point, not an official threshold. Below that, the tax savings of an SL rarely outweigh the extra cost of running one: mandatory bookkeeping, annual accounts filed with the Companies Registry, a more expensive accounting service.

## The reason that isn't about tax at all: liability

As a self-employed person, you're liable for the business's debts with your personal assets, present and future — with some protection for your primary residence under specific conditions, but as a general rule your personal wealth is exposed. With an SL, liability is generally limited to the capital contributed to the company. If your business carries real exposure to large debts — investment, suppliers, contracts with penalty clauses — this can outweigh any tax calculation, even at low turnover.

## The tax difference, in broad strokes

As self-employed, you pay income tax progressively: the more you earn, the higher the marginal rate on the top slice of your profit. An SL pays Corporate Tax at a flat rate, with a reduced rate for the first periods for newly created companies. The real comparison isn't just "the company rate versus the income tax rate" — you also need to factor in what it costs to take money out of the company for personal use, whether through salary or dividends, each taxed differently. It's not a calculation worth doing by eye.
